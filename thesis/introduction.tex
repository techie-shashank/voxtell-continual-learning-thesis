\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Medical image segmentation assigns every voxel of an image either to a structure, such as an organ or a tissue, or to the background.
The voxels assigned to a structure form its mask.
Masks can be drawn by hand.
However, drawing them is laborious, since in a three-dimensional (3D) volume the structure has to be outlined on every slice in which it appears.
Masks of the same structure also differ between readers~\cite{desai2021skmtea}.

Automatic segmentation with deep learning has commonly meant training one model for each dataset and its target structures, for example with nnU-Net~\cite{isensee2018nnunetselfadaptingframeworkunetbased}.
Universal segmentation models replace these per-dataset models with one network that segments many structures.
For instance, the developers of the universal model SAT compared it with 72 nnU-Nets, one trained on each of their datasets~\cite{zhao2025sat}.
In such models, the structure to segment is specified by an additional input, the prompt~\cite{zhao2025sat,rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.
This prompt can be spatial, such as a point or a box marked in the image~\cite{Ma_2024,cheng2023sammed2d}, or text that names the target.

Text-promptable models differ in the text they accept.
SAT is prompted with terms from a fixed vocabulary~\cite{zhao2025sat}, whereas VoxTell accepts both a single anatomical term and a full clinical sentence~\cite{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.
We call models of the latter kind \emph{free-text promptable}.
In both models, however, the structure to segment is not tied to an output channel of the network.
Instead, the same weights serve every structure, and the text prompt alone selects the target~\cite{zhao2025sat,rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.

This thesis adapts VoxTell, which was trained on more than 62{,}000 \gls{ct}, \gls{mri}, and \gls{pet} volumes~\cite{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.
On datasets it was not trained on, VoxTell reached a mean \gls{dsc} of 70.85\,\%~\cite[Table~1]{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}, where the \gls{dsc} measures the overlap between the predicted and the reference mask.
On knee \gls{mri} from the SKM-TEA dataset~\cite{desai2021skmtea}, however, its mean \gls{dsc} was only 10.72\,\% (averaged over four cartilages and two menisci)~\cite[Suppl.~Table~9]{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.
The VoxTell authors attribute this low value to the small share of knee images in the training data and to structures missing from it.
As future work, they suggest adapting the model with a few labelled examples~\cite[Sec.~6]{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.

Automatic segmentation of the knee cartilages and menisci is useful because quantitative biomarkers for osteoarthritis are measured within these structures~\cite{desai2021skmtea}.
One example is the T2 relaxation time, which reflects the degeneration of collagen-rich tissue~\cite[Sec.~2]{desai2021skmtea}.
These values are measured within the segmented structures.
The SKM-TEA authors name manual outlining as one obstacle to the use of quantitative \gls{mri}~\cite[Sec.~2]{desai2021skmtea}.

With few manual outlines available, VoxTell would have to learn the knee structures from a few labelled volumes.
New structures may also become relevant over time, and the model would then have to adapt to each of them in turn.
Retraining VoxTell from scratch on its training data together with each new structure would be expected to preserve most of what it could segment before.
However, training the published model took about six days on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs~\cite[Sec.~5]{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal}.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

Instead of being retrained from scratch, the model can be adapted to one new structure at a time.
We call the learning of one new structure, named by one text prompt, a \emph{task}.
In the task-incremental setting of continual learning, a model learns from a sequence of tasks, using the data of the current task and limited or no data of earlier tasks~\cite[Sec.~2]{delange2022}.
After each task, the model has to segment the new structure while keeping the structures learned earlier in the sequence.
It also has to keep what it could segment before the first task, which we call its pretrained ability (\cref{fig:ch1_setting}).

The simplest way to learn a task is to fine-tune the model on its data.
However, training on later tasks can lower the performance on earlier ones, an effect known as catastrophic forgetting~\cite[Sec.~1]{delange2022}.
For pretrained vision--language models, continual fine-tuning can also lower the zero-shot accuracy on data the model was not trained on~\cite{zheng2023preventingzeroshottransferdegradation}.
In our setting, such a lowering corresponds to a loss of pretrained ability.
The more the model is allowed to change, the better it can learn the new structure, but the more it may lose of what it knew.
De Lange et al.\ call this conflict the stability--plasticity dilemma, with plasticity ``referring to the ability of integrating new knowledge'' and stability ``retaining previous knowledge while encoding it''~\cite[Sec.~1]{delange2022}.

With few labelled volumes per task, it is not clear in advance how the balance between stability and plasticity shifts.
When the number of training epochs is fixed, fewer volumes mean fewer updates.
On the one hand, fewer updates give the model less opportunity to learn the new structure.
On the other hand, they may change less of what it knew.
In this thesis, \emph{few-shot} means training the model on $k$ labelled volumes per task, where one shot is one labelled training volume.

The continual-learning results we found do not show how few labelled volumes shift the balance between stability and plasticity, because they were obtained in other settings.
The methods for pretrained vision--language models reviewed in \cref{sec:cl-vlm} were developed and evaluated on classification or on tasks with text output.
In medical image segmentation, many continual-learning methods give each new task or class components of its own, such as a decoder or an output head~\cite{ji2023continualsegmentsingleunified,guo2025continuallearningdrivenmodelaccurate,zhang2023continuallearningabdominalmultiorgan,ye2025medseqft}.
In contrast, VoxTell needs no such components, since a new structure is named by a new text prompt and segmented with the same weights.

In our literature search, we found no work that adapts a pretrained free-text promptable 3D segmentation model to a sequence of new structures with few labelled volumes per task (\cref{sec:sota-summary}).
The closest works use either a two-dimensional (2D) model with full training sets~\cite{gao2026medcrpcl,gao2025paewc,gao2026medpeftcl} or a 3D model without text prompts~\cite{chen2026fewshotcontinuallearning3d}.
None of them measures how much of the pretrained ability is kept.
Other works measure it for CLIP~\cite{Zhang_2024_CVPR} and for segmentation models prompted with points or boxes~\cite{wang2025casam,wang2024sammed3dmoe}, and our evaluation builds on this idea (\cref{sec:intro-approach}).

\section{Research Question and Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-rq}

This thesis addresses the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{How do different continual-learning strategies trade off adaptation performance and catastrophic forgetting when adapting free-text promptable universal 3D medical image segmentation models under few-shot conditions?}
\end{quote}
By \emph{adaptation performance}, we mean how well the model segments a new structure right after it has been trained on it.
In the research question, \emph{forgetting} covers both the loss on earlier tasks of the sequence and the loss of pretrained ability.
As few-shot conditions, we use $k = 5$, 10, and 20 labelled volumes per task.
We read them against the full training split of 108 volumes per task, which serves as the reference setting and is the only one in which all strategies are compared.

We pursue four objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Develop an evaluation protocol for the setting of the research question that measures adaptation, the forgetting of earlier tasks, and the loss of pretrained ability separately.
  \item Compare continual-learning strategies with each other and with fine-tuning on each task in turn, and determine how they trade adaptation against forgetting.
  \item Investigate how the number of labelled volumes per task influences adaptation and forgetting, separating the effect of fewer volumes from that of fewer updates.
  \item Analyse how the order of the tasks and the rank of the LoRA adapters influence adaptation and forgetting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Approach and Scope}
\label{sec:intro-approach}

We study the research question with VoxTell~\cite{rokuss2025voxtellfreetextpromptableuniversal} as the pretrained model and six structures of the SKM-TEA knee \gls{mri} dataset~\cite{desai2021skmtea} as tasks.
These are four cartilages (patellar, femoral, medial tibial, and lateral tibial) and two menisci (medial and lateral).
To see whether the results depend on the order of the tasks, we train them in two task orders, an original order chosen at random and its reverse.
The lung in the chest \gls{ct} volumes of AeroPath~\cite{støverud2023aeropathairwaysegmentationbenchmark} serves as a \emph{control task}.
It is not trained on during the adaptation and is used only to measure how much of the pretrained ability is kept.
After each task, the model is tested on all six tasks and on the control task (\cref{sec:method-setting-protocol}).

We compare sequential fine-tuning, the baseline, with five continual-learning strategies.
Sequential fine-tuning trains all parameters on each task and adds nothing to reduce forgetting.
Three strategies are based on \gls{lora}~\cite{hu2022lora}, which keeps the pretrained weights frozen and trains only a low-rank update of them.
We call this update the adapter.
The number of parameters of the adapter grows with its rank~$r$.
Task-specific LoRA trains one adapter of rank $r = 4$ per task and selects it by the task identity at inference.
In contrast, shared LoRA trains one adapter of rank $r = 1024$ for all tasks, and shared LoRA with replay adds a memory of training samples from earlier tasks.
The prompt-tuning strategy (adapted from CPE-CLIP~\cite{D_Alessandro_2023}) also freezes the pretrained weights and trains learned prompt tokens together with a meta-net, a small network that shifts the encoded text prompt.
The meta-net is our addition, inspired by CoCoOp~\cite{zhou2022cocoop}.
The distillation-based strategy (adapted from ZSCL~\cite{zheng2023preventingzeroshottransferdegradation}) trains all parameters and distils the features of the frozen pretrained model into the adapted one.
Together, the five continual-learning strategies cover multimodal replay, cross-modal regularisation, and parameter-efficient adaptation, which are the three families of continual-learning methods for vision--language models~\cite[Sec.~4]{liu2026continuallearningvlmssurvey}.
All strategies except task-specific LoRA use one parameter set for all tasks.

All strategies are trained with the full training split.
Sequential fine-tuning, task-specific LoRA, and shared LoRA are also trained with $k = 5$, 10, and 20 volumes per task.
Further runs keep the number of updates of the full training split and so separate the effect of fewer volumes from that of fewer updates (\cref{subsec:exp-data}).

From the test results, we compute three primary metrics (\cref{sec:bg-metrics}): Adaptation, the \gls{dsc} on each task right after training on it; Final Forgetting~\cite{Chaudhry_2018}, the loss on the earlier tasks by the end of the sequence; and the control-task drop, the loss on the control task.
Together, Final Forgetting and the control-task drop measure retention, the performance kept on earlier tasks and on the control task.
Final Performance and Transfer~\cite{zheng2023preventingzeroshottransferdegradation} are secondary.
The pretrained model, evaluated in the same way, and joint fine-tuning, which trains the model on all six tasks at once, serve as references.

The study uses one model, one dataset for the tasks, one control task, one fixed text prompt per structure, and one draw of training volumes per~$k$.
Since each configuration was trained once, the only variability we can estimate is that over the test volumes (\cref{subsec:exp-metrics}).
Therefore, the conclusions are limited to this setting (\cref{sec:exp-limitations}).
Clinical deployment and studies with clinical users are outside the scope of this thesis, but we report the training cost of the strategies (\cref{sec:exp-compute}).

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}
% Contributions decided by the author on 2026-10-26 (option A, thesis_notes.md §1); one per objective since 2026-11-01.

The contributions of this thesis are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An evaluation protocol that measures the loss of pretrained ability separately from the forgetting of earlier tasks, through a held-out control task, with the pretrained model and joint fine-tuning as reference points (\cref{sec:method-setting-protocol}).
  \item A comparison of sequential fine-tuning and five continual-learning strategies adapted to VoxTell under one shared training configuration, together with a diagnosis of the failure of task-specific LoRA on patellar cartilage (\cref{sec:method-strategies,sec:exp-main,sec:exp-patellar}).
  \item An analysis of how the amount of labelled data affects adaptation and forgetting, with three of the strategies and joint fine-tuning. It separates fewer volumes from fewer updates by training on five volumes with as many updates as on the full training split (\cref{sec:exp-few-shot}).
  \item An analysis of how the task order and the rank of the LoRA adapters affect adaptation and forgetting, with two task orders and ranks from $r = 4$ to $r = 2048$ (\cref{sec:exp-rank,sec:exp-task-order}).
\end{enumerate}

Sequential fine-tuning, LoRA, replay~\cite[Sec.~3.1]{delange2022}, ZSCL, and CPE-CLIP are existing methods, and the metrics and the idea of a held-out control set come from prior work (\cref{sec:bg-metrics}).
Our work consists of the application of these methods to VoxTell with the changes described in \cref{sec:method-strategies}, the evaluation protocol that combines these metrics with a control task and a pretrained-model reference, and all experiments.
The works reviewed in \cref{sec:sota-summary} do not study the setting of \cref{sec:intro-problem}.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

\Cref{chap:sota} reviews text-promptable medical image segmentation and few-shot adaptation of segmentation models, as well as continual learning for pretrained vision--language models and for medical image segmentation.
It ends with the gap that this thesis addresses.
\Cref{chap:background} introduces VoxTell, states the continual-learning problem in formal terms, describes the published methods on which the strategies are based, and defines the evaluation metrics.
\Cref{chap:data} describes the SKM-TEA and AeroPath data, the tasks, and the data splits.
\Cref{chap:method} defines the evaluation protocol, the adaptation of VoxTell to one task, and the strategies with their complexity.
\Cref{chap:experiments} gives the experimental setup and the results.
It compares the strategies, diagnoses the failure of task-specific LoRA on patellar cartilage, and reports the experiments on the amount of labelled data, the LoRA rank, and the task order, as well as the training cost.
The chapter ends with the limitations of the experiments and the results that may generalise beyond them.
\Cref{chap:summary} summarises the results, answers the research question, and outlines future work.
The appendix lists the training and test volumes and the parameters of all runs (\cref{sec:app-volumes,sec:app-params}) and reports the additional evaluations, including two components adopted from ZSCL and CPE-CLIP (\cref{sec:app-additional,sec:exp-components}).
It also gives the evaluation matrices of all runs and the training loss of shared LoRA (\cref{sec:app-matrices,sec:app-shared-loss}).
